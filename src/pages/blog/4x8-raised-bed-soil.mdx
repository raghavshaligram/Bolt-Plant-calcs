<p class="mb-5 text-base leading-relaxed text-bark-600 sm:text-lg">
  If you're wondering how much soil for a 4x8 raised bed you actually need, the short answer is <strong>32 cubic feet</strong> &mdash; about <strong>1.19 cubic yards</strong> &mdash; at the standard 12-inch depth. Go shallower and you need less: an 8-inch bed takes about 21.3 cubic feet. Go deeper and you need more: 18 inches takes a full 48 cubic feet. The table below breaks down the exact volume, in cubic feet, cubic yards, and bag count, for every depth gardeners actually build to.
</p>

<h2 id="how-to-fill-it">How to Fill It &mdash; And the Mistake Most People Make</h2>
<p>
  The most common shortcut advice for a raised bed is to save money by stuffing the bottom with logs, branches, or cardboard before adding soil on top &mdash; sometimes called hugelkultur, or lasagna-style bottom-filling. It's a real technique, but it's built for something different than what most people are using it for. Traditional hugelkultur mounds are two to three feet tall or more, built to develop over several years as buried wood slowly breaks down and feeds the soil above it. Washington State University Extension, reviewing the technique, found that even properly built hugelkultur mounds typically need to be <a href="https://pubs.extension.wsu.edu/product/hugelkultur-what-is-it-and-should-it-be-used-in-home-gardens/" rel="noopener">rebuilt from scratch every five to six years</a> as the decomposing wood collapses and the mound loses its shape.
</p>
<p>
  In a standard 10&ndash;12 inch raised bed, you get the downside without ever getting the payoff. Decomposing wood mixed into soil ties up nitrogen your plants need while microbes work to break it down &mdash; Oregon State University Extension's research on <a href="https://extension.oregonstate.edu/ask-extension/featured/wood-chips-mulch" rel="noopener">wood chips mixed into garden soil</a> found a measurably greater tendency to tie up nitrogen than the same material left as surface mulch &mdash; and the buried layer compresses as it breaks down, so the bed sinks well below its frame within a season or two. Fill the full depth with an actual soil mix instead. It costs more up front, but the bed is productive immediately instead of fighting a nitrogen deficit for a year.
</p>
<p>
  None of that means hugelkultur itself is a bad technique &mdash; it's a genuinely useful way to fill a bed that's 24 inches or deeper, where there's enough soil depth above the buried wood to keep roots away from the nitrogen-hungry decomposition zone, and enough time ahead of you to let a multi-year mound mature. The mismatch is specifically using it as a cost-saving shortcut in a standard-depth rectangular bed, where the wood ends up in the same root zone as this season's plants instead of well beneath it.
</p>

<p>
  Once the bottom-filling question is settled, the mix itself is simpler. The standard raised bed soil mix is <strong>60% topsoil, 30% compost, and 10% perlite or coarse sand</strong> for drainage &mdash; the same ratio our <a href="/calculators/raised-bed-soil-calculator/">Raised Bed Soil Calculator</a> uses to work out exactly how much of each component your bed needs. A 50/30/20 blend with more perlite or sand is a common alternative for heavier native soils, and Square Foot Gardening's well-known &ldquo;Mel's Mix&rdquo; skips topsoil entirely for equal thirds compost, peat moss or coco coir, and vermiculite. Any of the three works well; what actually hurts a bed is straight garden soil dug from the yard, which compacts hard and drains poorly once it's boxed in.
</p>
<p>
  Whatever mix you use, plan for it to settle. Loose soil typically loses <strong>10&ndash;20% of its volume</strong> in the first year alone, as organic matter breaks down, watering compacts loose material, and air pockets close up. Fill a couple of inches proud of the bed's frame when you first build it, and expect to top off with a bag or two each spring to hold the bed at full depth &mdash; that's normal maintenance, not a sign anything went wrong.
</p>

<h2 id="square-foot-gardening">Square Foot Gardening in a 4x8 Bed</h2>
<p>
  A 4&times;8 bed divides cleanly into <strong>32 one-foot squares</strong> &mdash; 4 across by 8 down &mdash; which is exactly the grid Square Foot Gardening is built around. Once you're thinking in squares instead of rows, the spacing figures from the table above translate directly: a plant that needs 4 square feet takes up 4 whole squares, which is why tomatoes work out to 8 plants total rather than 32. A plant that needs 1 square foot gets its own square, and small crops like carrots and radishes can be packed many to a single square. It's the same math as the layout table above, just organized by the grid instead of by the whole bed.
</p>
<p>
  The grid also matters for the soil question this whole guide starts with: because a square foot bed is planted so densely, it uses its full depth of soil productively square by square, instead of leaving gaps of bare soil between rows that a traditional layout would. That's part of why the method, popularized by Mel Bartholomew's original square foot gardening system, tends to get more harvest out of the same 32 cubic feet of soil than a conventional row layout in the same footprint.
</p>
