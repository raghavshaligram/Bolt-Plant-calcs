<h2 id="what-blossom-end-rot-actually-is">What Blossom End Rot Actually Is</h2>
<p>
  Tomato blossom end rot is the version most gardeners encounter first, but the same disorder shows up on peppers, squash, and watermelon fruit as well. Blossom end rot is a physiological disorder, not a disease -- no pathogen is involved, and it isn't contagious to other plants. Per the University of Wisconsin-Madison's Plant Disease Diagnostics Clinic, it shows up as decayed, sunken areas on the bottom (blossom end) of the fruit, and it's caused by a localized calcium deficiency in the developing fruit tissue itself. That's the part that trips people up: a "calcium deficiency" sounds like a soil problem you fix by adding more calcium, but it's almost always a delivery problem instead. Calcium moves through a plant mainly with water, riding along in the transpiration stream, and it doesn't travel back out of leaves once it's there. If water delivery to the fruit is inconsistent, even a fruit growing in calcium-rich soil can end up short on calcium right where it's needed most.
</p>

<h2 id="the-calcium-myth-why-spraying-doesnt-usually-work">The Calcium Myth: Why Spraying Doesn't Usually Work</h2>
<p>
  Reaching for calcium for blossom end rot is the instinctive move, and it's exactly the wrong one in most cases. This is the most persistent piece of bad gardening advice around blossom end rot, and it's worth addressing directly: foliar calcium sprays, and folk remedies like crushed Tums or eggshells worked into the soil, generally don't fix an active problem. According to Clemson Cooperative Extension's Home &amp; Garden Information Center, the skin of the fruit can't effectively absorb calcium sprayed onto it, and calcium that does reach the leaves can't move through the plant's phloem into the fruit -- calcium is what's called an immobile nutrient once it's deposited in plant tissue. Clemson also makes the more important point: blossom end rot can happen even when soil calcium levels are perfectly adequate, because the underlying problem usually isn't a shortage of calcium in the ground. It's a shortage of calcium reaching the fruit, which comes down to water.
</p>
<p>
  Eggshells and Tums aren't harmful additions to garden soil, but treat them as a slow, marginal soil amendment at best -- not a fix for fruit that's already showing symptoms. If you want to know for certain whether your soil is actually calcium-deficient rather than guessing, <a href="/blog/how-to-read-soil-test-results/">an actual soil test</a> will tell you far more reliably than a spray bottle will. The real blossom end rot treatment starts with watering consistency, not with adding more calcium.
</p>

<h2 id="the-real-cause-inconsistent-watering">The Real Cause: Inconsistent Watering</h2>
<p>
  Per Clemson HGIC and the Iowa State University Yard and Garden program, the most effective way to prevent blossom end rot is maintaining uniform soil moisture -- avoiding the swing between bone-dry and soaking wet that disrupts calcium delivery to developing fruit. Fluctuating soil moisture from overwatering, drought, or an inconsistent watering schedule is the condition most closely tied to blossom end rot showing up, especially early in the season when fruit is expanding fastest and demand for water (and the calcium riding along with it) is highest.
</p>
<p>
  The fix is almost entirely about consistency rather than volume: a drip irrigation system on a timer, or at minimum a fixed watering schedule rather than watering only when you remember, does more for blossom end rot than any spray. If you're not sure your current routine is actually consistent, <a href="/clusters/watering-and-irrigation/">our watering and irrigation calculators</a> can help you turn "water it sometimes" into an actual schedule based on your plant, pot or bed size, and climate.
</p>

<h2 id="which-plants-get-it">Which Plants Get It</h2>
<p>
  Blossom end rot on tomatoes (<em>Solanum lycopersicum</em>) is the most common version people search for, but it's a fruit-development issue that affects several other vegetables too, especially in the same growing conditions that stress tomatoes. Zucchini and other summer squash (<em>Cucurbita pepo</em>) are prone to it, often showing up as a dark, sunken patch at the blossom end that's easy to mistake for rot from contact with wet soil. Zucchini blossom end rot often gets mistaken for that kind of contact damage since the fruit sits low to the ground, but it's the same inconsistent-watering cause as on tomatoes. Blossom end rot peppers are common too. Bell peppers (<em>Capsicum annuum</em>) get it as well -- per the UF/IFAS Extension factsheet on bell pepper blossom-end rot, symptoms start as a light green or yellowish sunken spot that expands and eventually turns black as saprophytic fungi colonize the already-dead tissue, which is a secondary effect, not the original cause. Watermelon (<em>Citrullus lanatus</em>) can also develop it, particularly during hot, dry stretches when water demand spikes and watering tends to fall behind.
</p>
<p>
  Across all of these, the trigger is the same: rapidly expanding fruit tissue with an inconsistent water supply, not a plant-specific quirk.
</p>

<h2 id="how-to-actually-fix-and-prevent-it">How to Actually Fix and Prevent It</h2>
<p>
  For gardeners specifically wondering how to prevent blossom end rot on tomatoes, the checklist below covers everything that actually works. How you treat blossom end rot and how you prevent blossom end rot from coming back are really the same short list -- there's no separate "cure" beyond removing already-affected fruit and fixing the conditions that caused it.
</p>
<p>
  <strong>Water consistently.</strong> This is the single biggest lever. Aim for roughly 1 to 1½ inches of water per week for tomatoes during the growing season, per Iowa State University Extension, delivered on a regular schedule rather than in occasional heavy soakings after the soil has already gone dry.
</p>
<p>
  <strong>Mulch around the base of the plant.</strong> A layer of mulch or pine straw slows evaporation and buffers the soil against the wet-dry swings that disrupt calcium delivery -- both Clemson HGIC and Wisconsin Extension name this as a standard prevention step alongside consistent watering.
</p>

<p>
  <strong>Don't over-fertilize with nitrogen.</strong> Heavy nitrogen fertilization pushes fast, leafy growth that outpaces root development and water uptake, which can make blossom end rot worse rather than better. If you're unsure what your soil actually needs, <a href="/clusters/fertilizer-and-nutrients/">our fertilizer and nutrient calculators</a> can help you feed based on an actual rate instead of a habit.
</p>
<p>
  <strong>Skip the extra calcium.</strong> Per Iowa State University Extension, most garden soils -- Iowa's included -- already contain more than adequate calcium, so adding more to the soil rarely addresses the actual bottleneck. If you want to confirm rather than guess, <a href="/blog/how-to-read-soil-test-results/">reading an actual soil test</a> will tell you what your soil truly has and needs.
</p>
<p>
  <strong>Remove affected fruit.</strong> Fruit that already shows blossom end rot won't recover. Picking and discarding it lets the plant put its energy into the fruit still developing, per Iowa State University Extension.
</p>

<h2 id="can-you-eat-the-rest-of-the-fruit">Can You Eat the Rest of the Fruit?</h2>
<p>
  In most cases, yes. Blossom end rot is a physiological disorder, not a pathogen, so it isn't a food-safety hazard on its own. If the affected area is small, firm, and dry-looking, many gardeners simply cut away the damaged section and eat the rest of the fruit normally. That said, use ordinary judgment: if the spot has become soft, mushy, or shows the fuzzy growth of a secondary mold or fungal infection moving in on the dead tissue -- which can happen once the fruit's skin has broken down -- it's best to discard that fruit rather than eat around it. When in doubt, or if a large portion of the fruit is affected, throwing it out is the safer call.
</p>
