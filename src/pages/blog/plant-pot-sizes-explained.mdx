<p class="mb-5 text-base leading-relaxed text-bark-600 sm:text-lg">
  So how are plant pots measured, exactly? Not by a ruler across the top, most of the time &mdash; nursery pot sizes are usually sold in "gallons," a trade-size label that describes roughly how much soil the pot holds, not its exact volume or its diameter. That's why a "5-gallon" pot from one brand can look noticeably different from another, and why two pots with the same width can hold very different amounts of soil depending on how deep they are. This guide gives you the real gallon-to-inches numbers &mdash; diameter <em>and</em> height, which most charts skip &mdash; plus how to measure a pot yourself and how to tell when a plant has actually outgrown its container.
</p>

<h2 id="how-are-plant-pots-actually-measured">How Are Plant Pots Actually Measured?</h2>
<p>
  Nursery pot sizes trace back to how growers have sold plants for decades: by the trade size of the container, not its literal capacity. A "1-gallon" pot is the industry's name for a specific, roughly standardized pot &mdash; not a promise that it holds exactly one US gallon of soil. In practice, a 1-gallon nursery pot typically holds closer to 0.7&ndash;0.8 real gallons, because the trade-size number was set generations ago as a rough category, not a measured volume, and it stuck.
</p>
<p>
  Two things make this genuinely confusing if you've never measured a pot yourself. First, there's no single enforced industry standard &mdash; manufacturers each mold their own pots, so a "10-gallon" from one company and a "10-gallon" from another can differ by an inch or more in either dimension. Second, diameter alone doesn't tell you volume: a wide, shallow pot and a narrow, tall one can share the same top width and still hold very different amounts of soil, because height is doing real work that a single "size" number hides. That's exactly why the chart below lists both dimensions instead of just one.
</p>

<h2 id="how-to-measure-a-pot-yourself">How to Measure a Pot Yourself</h2>
<img
  src="/tape-measure-pot-diameter-measurement.webp"
  alt="Tape measure across the top diameter of a plant pot"
  width="1000"
  height="750"
  loading="lazy"
  class="my-6 w-full rounded-2xl border border-moss-100"
/>
<p>
  Charts and trade labels only get you so far &mdash; if you already own the pot in question, here's how to measure plant pots yourself in under a minute, no guessing required.
</p>
<ol>
  <li><strong>Diameter:</strong> lay a tape measure straight across the widest point of the pot's <em>top opening</em>, rim to rim &mdash; not the base, which is almost always narrower on a tapered pot. Round pots taper inward toward the bottom by design, so the top measurement is the one that matters for "will this fit next to my other pots" and for matching a trade size.</li>
  <li><strong>Height:</strong> measure straight down the outside of the pot, from the rim to the base. Skip this step and you'll only ever have half the picture &mdash; two pots with an identical diameter can differ by several inches in height, which is exactly the gap this guide's chart fills in.</li>
  <li><strong>Volume, if you need it:</strong> once you have both measurements, our <a href="/calculators/pot-size-calculator/">Pot Size Calculator</a> converts custom diameter-and-height numbers directly into gallons and liters, so you don't have to do the cylinder math by hand.</li>
</ol>
<p>
  A tape measure beats trusting the size printed on a nursery tag, because that number is a trade label, not a guarantee &mdash; and it's the only way to know for certain whether a pot you already own will actually fit the spot (or the plant) you have in mind.
</p>

<h2 id="when-to-actually-size-up">When to Actually Size Up</h2>
<p>
  Knowing a pot's dimensions only answers half the question. The other half &mdash; how to know when to repot a plant in the first place &mdash; is really about reading the plant, not the calendar. Here's what to actually check, in the order it's usually easiest to notice:
</p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Lift the plant out and look.</strong> This is the single most reliable check, and the one most guides skip because it takes more effort than glancing at the surface. Gently slide the root ball out of the pot (water it first so it releases cleanly). If you see a dense mat of roots circling the outside of the root ball, or roots that have taken on the pot's shape rather than the soil's, it's time to size up &mdash; you're looking at the plant's actual root system, not a proxy for it.</li>
  <li><strong>Check the drainage holes.</strong> Roots emerging from the bottom of the pot are an easier-to-spot version of the same signal, without needing to unpot anything.</li>
  <li><strong>Watch how fast water disappears.</strong> A root-bound plant has displaced most of its soil with roots, so water runs straight through and out the bottom within seconds, rather than being absorbed and held. If you're watering more often than you used to just to keep the plant from wilting, this is usually why.</li>
  <li><strong>Notice growth that's stalled for no other reason.</strong> If light, water, and season all check out normal but the plant simply isn't producing new growth during what should be its active season, a maxed-out root system is a common, easy-to-miss cause &mdash; especially compared to more visible problems like pests or under-watering.</li>
  <li><strong>Watch for a top-heavy plant.</strong> A plant that has outgrown its container's footprint above the soil line, to the point that it tips or leans without support, has usually also outgrown it below the soil line.</li>
</ul>

  src="/plant-roots-circling-root-ball-repotting.webp"

  width="1000"
  height="750"
  loading="lazy"
  class="my-6 w-full rounded-2xl border border-moss-100"
/>
<p>
  When you do size up, resist the urge to jump straight to a much bigger pot "to save a step." Slow growers &mdash; most succulents, cacti, and compact houseplants &mdash; do best moving up gradually, about 1&ndash;2 inches of diameter at a time. Fast, vigorous growers, and fast-cycling edibles started from seed, can usually handle a bigger jump of 2&ndash;3 inches, or in some cases straight to their mature minimum size in a single move. Either way, a container that's far larger than the current root system holds more wet soil than the roots can use between waterings &mdash; and that excess moisture sitting around underused roots is one of the most common causes of root rot, not a shortcut that saves you future repotting.
</p>
