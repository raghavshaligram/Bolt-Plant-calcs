<p class="mb-5 text-base leading-relaxed text-bark-600 sm:text-lg">
  Why are my plant leaves turning yellow? It's one of the most-searched questions in gardening, and the honest answer is that yellowing plant leaves can mean a dozen different causes that look nearly identical at first glance -- nutrient deficiencies, overwatering, natural aging, and more. The trick is that *where* the yellowing shows up on the plant, and how it's distributed across each leaf, narrows it down fast -- match the pattern below, then use <a href="/clusters/fertilizer-and-nutrients/">our fertilizer and nutrient calculators</a> to act on it once you know the cause. And if your plant is wilting as well as yellowing, see our <a href="/blog/why-is-my-plant-wilting/">guide to diagnosing why a plant is wilting</a> for that side of it.

<h2 id="quick-answer-why-are-my-plant-leaves-turning-yellow">Quick Answer: Why Are My Plant Leaves Turning Yellow?</h2>
<p>
  Understanding why leaves turn yellow starts with two questions, and answering them is the fastest path to a real answer -- faster than scrolling through photos hoping one looks familiar.
</p>
<div class="not-prose my-6 rounded-2xl border border-moss-200 bg-moss-50/60 p-6">
  <ol class="space-y-3 text-bark-700">
    <li><strong>1. Which leaves are yellowing -- old growth or new growth?</strong> Old, lower leaves point to a <em>mobile</em> nutrient running short and being pulled toward new growth. New, upper leaves point to an <em>immobile</em> nutrient that can't be relocated once it's used.</li>
    <li><strong>2. Is the whole leaf yellow, or just the tissue between the veins?</strong> Uniform yellowing (the whole leaf, veins included) usually means nitrogen. Yellow tissue with the veins staying green -- called interveinal chlorosis -- points to magnesium, iron, or manganese instead.</li>
  </ol>
  <p class="mt-4 text-sm text-bark-500">Answer both, then jump to the matching section below. If neither fits -- soft stems, wet soil, or a recent move -- skip to <a href="#not-a-nutrient-problem-other-causes">Other Causes</a>.</p>
</div>

<h2 id="old-leaves-yellowing-first-mobile-nutrients">Old Leaves Yellowing First: Mobile Nutrient Deficiencies</h2>
<p>
  Nitrogen, magnesium, and potassium are all <strong>mobile</strong> within the plant -- when supply runs short, the plant pulls these nutrients out of older leaves and relocates them to new growth, sacrificing the old leaf to keep the growing tip fed. That's why deficiency in any of these three shows up on the <strong>oldest leaves first</strong>, per Cornell University's Cooperative Extension.
</p>
<h3>Nitrogen Deficiency</h3>
<p>
  The most common cause of plant leaves turning yellow. Yellowing of leaves is due to deficiency of nitrogen more often than any other single nutrient problem: uniform yellowing -- the whole leaf, veins included -- starting on the lowest, oldest leaves and working upward as it worsens. Per the University of Maryland Extension, affected leaves are also smaller than normal and the plant looks generally pale and slow. The fix is a balanced or nitrogen-heavy feed timed to the plant's active growing season -- our <a href="/calculators/npk-calculator/">NPK Calculator</a> turns that into an actual application rate instead of a guess, or browse all of <a href="/clusters/fertilizer-and-nutrients/">our fertilizer and nutrient calculators</a> if you're not sure which one you need.
</p>
<h3>Magnesium Deficiency</h3>
<p>
  Interveinal yellowing specifically on <strong>older</strong> leaves -- the veins stay green while the tissue between them yellows, and per the University of Maryland Extension it can progress to reddish-purple blotches and browning at the leaf margins. Common in tomatoes (<em>Solanum lycopersicum</em>) and roses (<em>Rosa</em> spp.), both heavy feeders in soil that's often been depleted of magnesium over a season. Epsom salt (magnesium sulfate) is the standard fix, dissolved in water and applied to the soil.
</p>
<h3>Potassium Deficiency</h3>
<p>
  Yellowing that shows up at the leaf margins and tips first on older leaves, often followed by browning or scorching along the same edges as it progresses -- per the University of Maryland Extension, leaves may also crinkle or roll upward. A potassium-rich fertilizer corrects it; a <a href="/blog/how-to-read-soil-test-results/">soil test</a> confirms it's actually potassium and not one of the other two before you buy anything.
</p>

<h2 id="new-leaves-yellowing-first-immobile-nutrients">New Leaves Yellowing First: Immobile Nutrient Deficiencies</h2>
<p>
  Iron, manganese, calcium, and sulfur are the opposite case: they're largely <strong>immobile</strong> once the plant has used them, so it can't strip them out of old leaves to feed new ones. Deficiency in any of these shows up on the <strong>newest growth first</strong> instead, per Cornell's Cooperative Extension -- often the clearest single tell that separates this group from the mobile nutrients above.
</p>
<h3>Iron Chlorosis</h3>
<p>
  Interveinal yellowing on new growth specifically, with the veins staying visibly green -- per Illinois Extension, this is the classic presentation of iron chlorosis, and it's almost always a soil pH problem rather than an actual shortage of iron in the ground. Iron becomes chemically locked up and unavailable to roots once soil pH climbs much above 6.5-7.0, even when there's plenty physically present in the soil. See our <a href="/blog/how-to-read-soil-test-results/#understanding-your-soil-ph-number">guide to reading your soil pH number</a> before reaching for an iron supplement -- correcting the pH is usually the real fix, not just adding more iron on top of soil that will lock it up too.
</p>
<h3>Manganese Deficiency</h3>
<p>
  A similar interveinal pattern on new growth, less common than iron chlorosis but easy to confuse with it. Per the University of Maryland Extension, manganese-deficient leaves tend to show darker green banding right along the veins rather than the crisp green-vein-on-yellow contrast of iron. A soil test is the reliable way to tell them apart before treating.
</p>
<h3>Calcium and Sulfur Deficiency</h3>
<p>
  Both are genuinely rare in home gardens with reasonable soil, and both are also immobile, showing on new growth -- calcium deficiency as small, distorted new leaves with dead tips (blossom end rot on tomatoes and peppers (<em>Capsicum annuum</em>) is the same underlying mechanism, just in the fruit instead of the leaf); sulfur deficiency as a pale, uniform yellowing on new growth that can look like nitrogen deficiency until you notice it's hitting the top of the plant instead of the bottom.
</p>

<h2 id="not-a-nutrient-problem-other-causes">Not a Nutrient Problem: Other Causes of Yellow Leaves</h2>
<h3>Do Yellow Leaves Mean Too Much Water?</h3>
<p>
  Often, yes. Per the Royal Horticultural Society, overwatered plants typically show <strong>old and new leaves yellowing at the same time</strong> -- not the clean old-leaf/new-leaf split of a nutrient deficiency -- plus soft or mushy stems, and roots that feel waterlogged rather than firm. Saturated soil pushes oxygen away from the root zone, and roots that can't get oxygen struggle to take up nutrients even when the soil has plenty, which is why overwatering can produce yellowing that looks confusingly like a deficiency. The fix is letting the soil dry out between waterings and improving drainage; our <a href="/calculators/soil-type-calculator/">Soil Type Calculator</a> can help confirm whether heavy, slow-draining soil is working against you, and it's worth taking a moment to <a href="/clusters/watering-and-irrigation/">check your watering schedule</a> against the plant's actual needs rather than a fixed routine. And <a href="/blog/why-is-my-plant-wilting/">if your plant is wilting as well as yellowing</a>, our dedicated wilting diagnostic guide covers that overlap in more depth.
</p>

<h3>Underwatering</h3>
<p>
  The contrast case: yellowing paired with leaves that feel dry, crispy, or thin rather than soft, and soil that's pulled away from the pot edge or cracked at the surface. Consistent, deep watering on a real schedule fixes it faster than a single heavy soak. Our <a href="/calculators/drip-irrigation-calculator/">Drip Irrigation Calculator</a> works out a consistent run time for an in-ground bed.
</p>
<h3>Natural Aging</h3>
<p>
  A few of the lowest, oldest leaves yellowing and dropping occasionally, with the rest of the plant green and vigorous, is completely normal. Plants shed leaves that have already done their job to redirect energy toward new growth. This is not a problem to fix -- it's worth ruling out first before assuming something's actually wrong. And if it's progressed to browning rather than staying yellow, see our <a href="/blog/why-are-my-plant-leaf-tips-turning-brown/">guide to diagnosing brown leaf tips</a> for that side of it.
</p>
<h3>Transplant or Root Shock</h3>
<p>
  Yellowing shortly after a move -- repotting, transplanting outdoors, or a big environmental change -- is usually stress, not disease or deficiency. Roots damaged or disturbed during the move take time to recover function. Most plants bounce back within a week or two with consistent (not excessive) watering and no fertilizer until new growth resumes.
</p>
<h3>Pest or Disease</h3>
<p>
  Sap-sucking pests (spider mites, aphids, scale) and fungal or viral diseases can both cause yellowing, usually alongside other tells -- stippling, webbing, sticky residue, spots, or curling that a pure nutrient or watering issue won't produce. If the yellowing comes with visible insects, holes, or leaf spots rather than a clean pattern, treat it as a pest or disease question rather than a feeding or watering one -- we'll cover specific pest and disease diagnosis in future guides.
</p>

<h2 id="does-it-matter-if-its-an-indoor-or-outdoor-plant">Does It Matter If It's an Indoor or Outdoor Plant?</h2>
<p>
  Outdoor plant leaves turning yellow and indoor plant leaves turning yellow usually trace back to the same underlying causes above, but the odds shift depending on where the plant lives.
</p>
<h3>Indoor Plants</h3>
<p>
  Overwatering is the single most common cause indoors, usually made worse by a pot with no drainage hole or a saucer that lets the roots sit in standing water. Low light can also mimic a nutrient deficiency -- a plant that isn't getting enough light to use the nutrients it has will yellow and stretch even on a normal feeding schedule. Dry, heated indoor air in winter adds a third variable, stressing plants that would otherwise be fine.
</p>
<h3>Outdoor Plants</h3>
<p>
  Outdoor plant leaves turning yellow more often trace back to soil and weather: heavy rain can leach nitrogen and other mobile nutrients out of the root zone within days, soil pH can swing enough after a season of amendments to lock up iron or manganese, and a sudden cold snap can trigger yellowing that looks like a deficiency but is really just cold stress.
</p>
